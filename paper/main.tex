\DraftBox
Companies and public bodies are adopting language models to triage and decide cases. Decisions often depend upon who a
party is: interns have different responsibilities to managers; householders owe insurers less disclosure than
businesses. We took \NumPairs{} matched pairs of cases from \NumDomains{} domains, read side by side and alone by
\NumConfigurations{} configurations of \NumDistinctModels{} models, with and without reasoning. Asked directly, models
say parties should be held to different standards. Side by side they apply the difference. But real-world deployments
evaluate single cases. Given one case, models mostly do not apply it. Without reasoning, they differentiate the parties
in \AloneNoReasoning{} of pairs, against \SideBySideNoReasoning{} side by side. Instead, they judge both parties by the
stricter standard: where the requirement needs judgement, they fail the junior analyst or the ten-year-old in
\HeldNotMetAloneNoReasoningJudgement{} of single cases, including \HeldNotMetAloneSidePassedNoReasoningJudgement{} of
those they pass side by side. Reasoning recovers fixed rules but not requirements that need judgement. Obvious fixes
have costs: stating the principle helps little; a precise rule lets through failings it does not mention, or, asked
more broadly without reasoning, fails more householders and junior analysts; a worked example causes few new errors but
only addresses part of the problem.\ReleaseMark}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Many business, educational and administrative decisions turn on the nature of the party. For example, the same essay
receives higher marks if written by a ten-year-old rather than a seventeen-year-old; an intern should be judged
against lesser responsibilities than a senior manager; a householder will face a lower burden of disclosure to an
insurer than a major business. Language models recognise this differential in principle. They also recognise it when
comparing two such cases in context, but they mostly ignore it when they assess a case in isolation.

This is a live issue for production systems because models are tasked with assessing each case in isolation: the
usual unit of decision is one file. If a model reviews a conduct file on a junior analyst, no file on a senior manager
is provided as a reference point. When a ten-year-old's essay is marked, there is no essay from a seventeen-year-old
as a reference.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \input{figures/fig1_worked}
  \caption{One pair from the insurance domain. Shown both policyholders, \model{Claude Haiku 5.5 (thinking off)} holds
  the business to the stricter duty of disclosure and finds that the householder met the standard. Shown each file
  alone, the same model finds the standard not met for both: the householder is held to the business's duty.}
  \label{fig:worked}
\end{figure}
Use of AI is now near-universal across business and is growing. In a McKinsey global survey
\citep[\LitMcKinseyExhibit]{mckinsey2026state} this year, \LitMcKinseyRegularUse{} of respondents reported regular use of AI in at least one business function, and among
organisations using AI, \LitMcKinseyScaling{} reported that it is scaling across the enterprise. Using models to grade or
score unstructured material is a common use.

Our testing distinguishes between two kinds of requirement.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Fixed rules}: like a reporting duty that only begins at \LitReportingThreshold{} employees. These can be
mapped onto the facts of a case quite cleanly.
\item \emph{Requirements that need judgement}: like the responsibilities of a senior manager, a vulnerable customer's
rights or the grammatical standard expected of a ten-year-old. There is no mechanical solution in these cases.
\end{itemize}
The former requirements are easily reduced to rules; the latter are diffuse and cannot easily be summarised in rule
form \citep{kaplow1992rules}. Regulators often prefer broad principles to detailed rules, partly because detailed rules
invite ``creative compliance'': meeting the letter of a rule but not its spirit \citep{black2007making}. The FCA's
Consumer Duty is a recent example of a move to principles-based regulation.

The obvious ways to check that a model performs against a requirement are to ask it what the requirement is, or to
have it evaluate two cases side by side. Models consistently pass both tests. When models are asked directly whether
this requirement differs by party, for example whether a business should be held to a stricter duty of disclosure
than a householder, they say yes in \DirectYesRateNoReasoning{} of answers without reasoning and
\DirectYesRateReasoning{} with. Shown both cases side by side, they treat the parties differently in
\SideBySideNoReasoning{} of pairs without reasoning and \SideBySideReasoning{} with it. Given one file, they do so in
just \AloneNoReasoning{} and \AloneReasoning{}.

Figure~\ref{fig:worked} shows one example of one model separating parties side by side but not in the single file.
This failure is silent. It is not shown in tests looking for differential treatment. In this case, the treatment
should be differential. It can only be caught by re-running files with the party changed and comparing verdicts.

Our test set comprises \NumPairs{} matched pairs of short vignettes in \NumDomainsWord{} domains: school work,
insurance disclosure, statutory thresholds, seniority, dismissal, suitability of advice and vulnerable customers
(Table~\ref{tab:domains}).

The two cases in each pair list the same conduct and the same outcome, only the party differs: a householder vs.\ a
business, a junior analyst vs.\ a senior manager, a ten-year-old vs.\ a seventeen-year-old.

Each model judged whether the requirement had been met, with the two cases side by side and with each case on its
own. The case was read on its own in four ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item as written;
\item plus the requirement stated in principle (for example, ``A large employer is held to a higher standard of
procedure before dismissing someone than a small employer.'');
\item plus the requirement translated into a precise rule (for example, ``\ExampleRuleQuote{}'');
\item plus a worked example.
\end{itemize}
We also asked each model directly whether the requirement differs between the parties. We read
\NumConfigurations{} configurations of \NumDistinctModels{} models, with and without reasoning. While the particular
cases are set in UK law, the requirements they test have close counterparts in legal systems around the world.
Table~\ref{tab:domains} shows how the \NumDomainsWord{} domains map onto these uses.

\paragraph{Our findings, in summary.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Models know the requirement, but they do not apply it to individual cases.} When asked directly, models
say that parties should be held to different standards \DirectYesRateNoReasoning{}--\DirectYesRateReasoning{} of the
time. Shown both cases, models without reasoning split \SideBySideNoReasoning{} of pairs by verdict. Given only one
file, this falls to \AloneNoReasoning{}. The figures are \SideBySideReasoning{} versus \AloneReasoning{} with reasoning.
\item \textbf{When models fail, they over-blame.} Where a rule of law settles the answer, models without reasoning
wrongly fail the party the law holds to less in \HeldToLessWronglyFailedNoReasoning{} of cases. Where the requirement
needs judgement, their performance is worse. They fail the ``party held to less'' in
\HeldNotMetAloneNoReasoningJudgement{} of single files. Where the same model, shown both, had passed the case side by
side, \HeldNotMetAloneSidePassedNoReasoningJudgement{} are failed. In most domains, the party held to less is the
weaker one. In practice, the householder, the junior analyst, the small employer and the ten-year-old are held to a
tougher requirement that should only apply to a business, senior manager, large employer or seventeen-year-old.
\item \textbf{Reasoning fixes verdicts against rules, but not judgements.} Alone, reasoning models decide
\AloneReasoningFixedRule{} of fixed-rule pairs correctly. But they only split \AloneReasoningJudgement{} of judgement
pairs. This problem has not been solved in frontier models. The most advanced commercial model we tested
(\model{Claude Opus 5.5}) splits \SideBySideOpusFiveFiveDefaultEffortJudgement{} of judgement pairs side by side but
only splits \AloneOpusFiveFiveDefaultEffortJudgement{} alone.
\item \textbf{None of the standard developer fixes provides a full answer for requirements that need judgement.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The share of judgement pairs that split rises from \AloneNoReasoningJudgement{} to
\ToldPrincipleNoReasoningJudgement{} without reasoning, and from \AloneReasoningJudgement{} to
\ToldPrincipleReasoningJudgement{} with, when the principle is stated.
\item A precise rule fixes the verdict it addresses. But the usual question then lets through
\LetThroughNoReasoningJudgement{} and \LetThroughReasoningJudgement{} of failings it does not mention. Changing the
question to whether the party met all its obligations blocks this with reasoning. Without reasoning, models fail the
``party held to less'' instead.
\item A worked example with a counterexample lets almost no failings through. But it raises the share of judgement
pairs that split from \AloneNoReasoningJudgement{} to only \ExampleSplitNoReasoningJudgement{} without reasoning. With
reasoning, the impact is still modest, rising from \AloneReasoningJudgement{} to \ExampleSplitReasoningJudgement{} with.
\end{itemize}
\item \textbf{The usual tests do not reveal the problem.} A model passes when asked to state or size the principle, and
when comparing cases. This problem is invisible to standard outcome monitoring because it looks for wrongly unequal
treatment rather than wrongly equal treatment. The way to catch it is to re-run live files with the party swapped via
the production pipeline.
\end{itemize}

In exploratory runs on graded scores, the difference also shrank when a case was read alone for animals and for
objects from different eras (Supplementary~\ref{app:exploratory}). \citet{young2026blame} found the same pattern for the standard of reasonable care,
locating it in base models before post-training. Here we show the behaviour extends to regulated decisions, and test
typical developer fixes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
\citet{zheng2023judging} used an early generation of models such as \model{GPT-4} to score the output of other
models. The performance was strong, with an \LitZhengAgreement{} match to human judgement: about the degree to which
human judgements match each other. They also identified systematic biases, including: position bias, a tendency to
favour the first or second option; verbosity bias, a tendency to score more elaborate responses higher; and
self-enhancement bias, a judge favouring answers from its own model family. They reduced, but did not remove, these
biases by position swapping and chain-of-thought reasoning. \citet{wang2023fair} again find that placement influences
results. They recommend two strategies to address this: prompting a model to generate explanations, or evidence,
before assigning a score; and evaluating candidate responses across two runs with swapped orders and averaging the
scores.

\citet{jeong2025pairwise} show that such biases are exacerbated by pairwise evaluation, where models directly compare
two items and easily prioritise superficial attributes. The biases are reduced when an item is judged in isolation.
They propose making a model reason about each output separately first, then making the pairwise decision. Comparison
amplifies whatever differs between two items. In their examples the difference is superficial, so comparison
misleads; in our pairs it is the party, so comparison helps and its absence is the problem.

Closer to our approach, studies of demographic bias have found that how a judgement is set up can change a model's
verdict. \citet{nghiem2024doctor} found that models favoured a group when choosing one candidate from a list but offered
it lower salaries when assessing candidates one at a time. \citet{vohra2026audit} found that how an audit was
constructed, above all a candidate's position in a list, moved verdicts in hiring, lending and triage as much as
demographics did, while a reported reversal between question formats did not generalise.

Like ours, those studies hold the facts constant and change only the subject. But they ask whether parties who should
be treated alike are treated differently. Our study looks at the inverse failure: whether parties who should be
treated differently are being treated alike.

Well-known benchmarks of legal reasoning like LegalBench \citep{guha2023legalbench} test whether models know and apply
rules to single items with fixed answers. None holds the requirement and the facts fixed and varies only the party.

Similar behaviour regarding side-by-side vs.\ individual judgements has been found in humans. The evaluability
hypothesis \citep{hsee1996evaluability,hsee1999preference} explains why joint and separate evaluations give different
results: preferences reverse when one attribute is hard to evaluate alone and another is easy.

In one example, one job candidate is described as having written \LitHseeProgramsA{} programs in a programming
language in \LitHseeYears{} years and has a grade point average of \LitHseeGpaA{} on a five-point scale. The value of
this becomes clearer when set beside a second candidate who has written just \LitHseeProgramsB{} in the same period
but has a grade point average of \LitHseeGpaB{}. Evaluated alone, the second is preferred. Evaluated side by side, the
first \citep{hsee1996evaluability}. This has parallels with our work: a ten-year-old's essay is easier to rate with an
older student's essay beside it.

\citet{young2026blame} found a similar bias against the generic standard of reasonable care. The law is less
demanding of a private person than of a professional doing the same work. Models recognise this when asked about the
principle or when the two are compared. Scoring each
alone, however, most models withheld about half or more of the latitude they themselves stated a private person is owed. The behaviour is present
in pretrained base models, which suggests it comes from what models learn from text, not from how they are tuned for use.

\subsection{Cases}
Our tests consist of \NumPairs{} pairs of short vignettes that cover \NumDomainsWord{} domains (Table~\ref{tab:domains}).
Each pair describes the same conduct with the same outcome. They differ only in who, or what, the party is:
\begin{itemize}
\item a householder vs.\ a business buying insurance
\item an employer below vs.\ above a statutory size threshold
\item a junior analyst vs.\ a senior manager
\item a small vs.\ a large employer dismissing someone
\item a firm advising a professional vs.\ advising a retail client
\item a firm dealing with an ordinary customer vs.\ a vulnerable customer
\item a younger student vs.\ an older student.
\end{itemize}
In suitability of advice and vulnerable customers, the party judged is the firm, and the question names the firm
(``the standard that applies to this firm in advising this client'', ``in dealing with this customer''); what varies is
its client or customer. In each pair, the requirement demands more of one party than the other. We call the first party in each pair, the
party of whom less is required, the ``party held to less''.

There are two kinds of domain:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Fixed-rule cases}, i.e.\ insurance disclosure and statutory thresholds. Here, a rule of law sets the
correct verdict for each party. The case gives enough facts to decide it, e.g.\ the number of employees, but never
names the rule; \RepoWhere{} gives the legal provision for each pair.
\item \emph{Judgement cases}, i.e.\ seniority, dismissal, suitability of advice, vulnerable customers and school work.
Here, determining what is required in a particular case requires judgement, e.g.\ what conduct is expected of an
employee of a certain seniority in a particular situation.
\end{itemize}
The cases were designed to split the verdicts by meeting the lighter requirement but falling short of the heavier one,
i.e.\ the party held to less passes, the other party fails. In judgement cases, models comparing both cases side by side
split this way in \JudgementConsensusShare{} of pairs (Table~\ref{tab:judgement-consensus}). This measure is lowest in school work
(\EduConsensusShare{}). Models often hold a younger student to a higher standard than we expected.

\subsection{Conditions}
Each pair was read in seven ways. In every case the question was the same:
\begin{quote}
Judged by the standard that applies to [this party], was the standard met? Answer yes or no.
\end{quote}
We used a yes-or-no verdict rather than grading, because that is the ultimate form decisions take in the real world
(Table~\ref{tab:design}), e.g.\ pass/fail, sanction or no sanction, claim granted or rejected.

The first two readings test the central claim:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Side by side.} Both cases are presented in one prompt. They are presented in two orders, as position is
known to affect model judgement \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2023fair}.
\item \emph{Alone.} Each case in its own prompt.
\end{itemize}
The other five conditions recreate a developer's reaction to a failed test:
\begin{itemize}
\item add the requirement to the prompt
\item translate a diffuse requirement into a precise rule
\item show the model two decided cases (``worked examples''), one passing and one failing.
\end{itemize}
For the last two, we also check whether the fix introduces new errors of its own, i.e.\ whether it leads the model to
accept failures against the requirement that the fix does not target directly.

The tests take the following format:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Alone, told in principle.} One sentence is added before the case stating, in general terms, how the
requirement differs between parties, e.g.\ ``A large employer is held to a higher standard of procedure before
dismissing someone than a small employer.''
\item \emph{Alone, told a precise rule.} The requirement is translated into a precise rule that settles the
case by a fact the case states, e.g.\ ``\ExampleRuleQuote{}'' In fixed-rule cases, this is the real-world rule
stated in plain terms. In judgement cases, we stipulate a rule that falls within the requirement. None of the rules
overrides the law or the principle.
\item \emph{Alone, outside the rule.} The case of the party held to less is presented with an additional failing that
the rule does not cover. This is read alone with and without the rule. Half the failings concern the rule's own subject
and half another subject. Adding the rule should not impact the verdict.
\item \emph{Alone, with a worked example.} Before the case, two decided cases are added from the same domain as worked
examples. These come from a different pair to avoid simply showing the model the answer:
\begin{itemize}
\item the ``party held to less'' is shown as judged to have met the requirement, with a one-sentence reason; and
\item the same case with a further failing, judged not to have met it, with its reason.
\end{itemize}
For example, before a dismissal case, a small employer that had warned a gardener twice by word of mouth rather than in
writing before dismissing them was judged to have met the standard, with the reason ``Marsh Lane Nurseries is a small
employer, and a small employer may give warnings by word of mouth''. The same case in which the employer also told
other local employers an untruth about the gardener was judged not met.
\item \emph{Alone, outside the example.} The same as ``Alone, outside the rule'', but with the worked examples in place
of the precise rule.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Second wording.} To check how sensitive the results are to the wording of the question, we drew
\SecondWordingPairs{} pairs at random, two from each domain, and read the outside conditions again with the question:
\begin{quote}
``\ldots did the [party] meet all of [its/their] obligations?\ldots''
\end{quote}
The pronoun follows the party: ``its'' for a company, employer or firm, ``their'' for a policyholder, employee or student.
As controls, we also read the plain case with the rule, and the outside case without it.

\emph{Milder conduct.} When a model fails both parties alone, there are two possibilities. Either the model is
ignoring who the party is (the failure we test for), or its bar sits high enough that the conduct falls short for both
parties. So that we can tell the two apart, we read the pair again with the conduct made milder by one step, in a
version that at least two of three pilot models still split side by side.

\emph{Asked directly.} For each domain, we asked every model whether the party with the heavier requirement should be
held to a higher standard, for example: ``Should a large employer be held to more than a small employer when following
a procedure before dismissing someone? Answer yes or no''. We also asked to what degree it should be held to more, on
a scale from zero to nine to determine whether the yes-no output was driving the result.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}
We read \NumConfigurations{} configurations of \NumDistinctModels{} models through providers' interfaces. Of these,
\NumModelsNoReasoning{} were read without reasoning and \NumModelsReasoning{} with reasoning (Table~\ref{tab:models}).
Five models were read both ways.

The results were read over three sessions. The second (school work, the worked example, the direct question, the
second wording, milder conduct and the stated jurisdiction) was designed after the first session's results. School work and
its \NumPairsEducation{} pairs were read only in the second session. A brief third session tested whether the order of the
direct question impacted the result.

The first session's cases were re-read in the second session. This reproduced the first
(\ExampleBareSplitNoReasoningSixDomains{} and \ExampleBareSplitReasoningSixDomains{} of pairs split, against
\FirstSessionAloneNoReasoning{} and \FirstSessionAloneReasoning{}). This licenses pooling figures across the first two
sessions. For reference, the first session's design is in Table~\ref{tab:first-session}.

As models sample their answers, the same prompt can generate different answers. We submitted each prompt ten times to
models without reasoning and five times to models with reasoning. Each verdict is thus recorded as a rate, not as a
single value.

Five open models were initially read alone by log-probability, but they were later re-read by sampling so that every
condition uses one readout and numbers can be aggregated. No figure generated in the first session
(Table~\ref{tab:first-session}) changed by more than one point. No other pooled figure changed by more than three
points (Appendix~\ref{app:readout}).

When \model{Claude Haiku 5.5} was run at maximum effort, it reached its \TokenLimit{}-token limit without answering in
\HaikuMaxVoidCells{} of its \HaikuMaxCells{} cells (\HaikuMaxVoidShare{} of its reads). These have been excluded. If
we had included them as failures, it would have lowered the reasoning group's judgement-case figure side by side from
\VoidCheckExcludedSideBySideReasoningJudgement{} to \VoidCheckWrongSideBySideReasoningJudgement{}; the alone figure
would remain at \VoidCheckWrongAloneReasoningJudgement{} (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

By the time of the second session, \model{Kimi K3}'s provider had ceased to serve the model without reasoning. It was
read on another provider. Its second-session figures are reported apart in Appendix~\ref{app:later}. This is why
\model{Kimi K3} does not have school-work readings in the group without reasoning. It only contributes to the first
session's domains.

All prompts and responses are in \RepoWhere{}.

\subsection{Measure}
\label{sec:measure}
Our tests score pairs of verdicts rather than individual verdicts. A model that simply failed every party would still
get half of the single verdicts right even though it paid no attention at all to the nature of the party. It is only
by testing pairs that we can determine whether the nature of the party changes the verdict, which is the goal of the
test.

We say that a pair is ``split'' when the ``party held to less'' meets the requirement in at least \SplitHighPct{}\% of
answers while the other party meets it in at most \SplitLowPct{}\%. Side by side, the answers from the two orders are
pooled before this test. Where we count single files rather than pairs (the per-party figures in
\S\ref{sec:single-file} and Appendix~\ref{app:later}), a file is judged ``met'' when at least half of its answers say
yes.

For each condition (side by side, alone, told in principle, and so on), we report the percentage of pairs that split.
We give figures pooled across all models and for each domain. Each figure is presented with \ConfidenceLevel{}
intervals.

Each figure is impacted by two sources of uncertainty. They depend upon which cases we wrote and which models we
tested. The intervals were calculated by resampling. The interval covers the middle \ConfidenceLevel{} of results.

In fixed-rule cases, where a rule of law cleanly sets each verdict, a split pair is decided correctly and any other is
decided wrongly.

In judgement cases, we set the line between ``met'' and ``not met'' when designing the cases. We cannot call a pair
that does not split ``wrong'' on that basis. We ask instead whether the model's verdict alone is consistent with its
own view of the pair side by side. We also report another measure that does not depend on where we set the line: the
``party difference''. This is the share of ``yes'' answers for the party held to less minus that for the other party.
This matters because models can give both parties the same answer for two different reasons:
\begin{itemize}
\item it has set its own bar above or below both parties, or
\item it is ignoring who the party is.
\end{itemize}
We use each model's side-by-side answers to tell the two apart: for the pairs a model separates when it sees both cases
together, we ask whether it still separates them when it sees each case alone.

In the case of ``Alone, outside the rule'' and ``Alone, outside the example'', we say that an added failing is ``let
through'' when the model judges it not to meet the requirement without the precise rule or the worked examples, but
judges it to meet the requirement with the rule or worked example in place. The rule and the worked example have no
relevance to the added failing and should not impact the verdict. Here ``not met'' means at most \SplitLowPct{}\% of answers say yes, and
``met'' at least \SplitHighPct{}\%. The denominator is every model-pair cell in which both readings are valid, so a
failing already accepted without the fix counts as not let through.

As the \SplitHighPct{}:\SplitLowPct{} threshold is our choice, we checked the sensitivity of the results to this choice
by scoring every pair with a cut-off at \SingleCutoffPct{}\%. No figure changed by more than \CutoffMaxChange{} points
(Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

In \LowConsensusPairsCount{} judgement pairs (\LowConsensusPairsDomains{}), most reasoning models did not split the
parties even when presented side by side. Our verdict there is the most open to dispute. But leaving them out changes
no figure without reasoning by more than \LowConsensusMaxChangeNoReasoning{} points.

With reasoning, the judgement-case figures rise (side by side from \SideBySideReasoningJudgement{} to
\SideBySideReasoningJudgementExclLowConsensus{}, alone from \AloneReasoningJudgement{} to
\AloneReasoningJudgementExclLowConsensus{}), so the gap widens slightly. Thus, including these pairs makes our results
more conservative.

The results also hold without the \WeakSideModelsCount{} models that split fewer than half of all pairs even side by
side (\WeakSideModels{}). Without them, models without reasoning split \SideBySideNoReasoningStrongSide{} of pairs side
by side and \AloneNoReasoningStrongSide{} alone (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

\subsection{Controls}
Each domain had control pairs in which the two cases differed only in the day of the week. Across the
\ControlPairsTotal{} control pairs that were read alone, changing the day flipped \DayFlipAllControls{} of verdicts.
This flipped none at all side by side (\DayFlipFirstRunControlsSideBySideNum{} of
\DayFlipFirstRunControlsSideBySideDen{}).

Thus, an irrelevant change flips \DayFlipAllControls{} of verdicts. Most control verdicts were clear-cut. Where the
model's answers were divided, \DayFlipUnsettled{} flipped (Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}). A single verdict near the
threshold can therefore change by chance, but the differences between conditions that we report are far larger than
this.

To rule out the possibility that confusion over the legal regime drove the verdicts, we stated that ``UK law
applies.'' before the case. This changed no group figure. Fixed-rule pairs alone were split in
\UkLawSplitNoReasoningFixedRule{} of cases with the statement against \UkLawBaseSplitNoReasoningFixedRule{} without it
for models without reasoning. For models with reasoning, it was \UkLawSplitReasoningFixedRule{} with the statement and
\UkLawBaseSplitReasoningFixedRule{} without
(Appendix~\ref{app:later}).

\subsection{The party is lost on a single file}
\label{sec:single-file}
\textbf{Main contrast.} When models are shown both cases together, models without reasoning split
\SideBySideNoReasoning{} of pairs in line with the requirement. Shown each case on its own, they split just
\AloneNoReasoning{} (Table~\ref{tab:main}). With reasoning, the figures rise to \SideBySideReasoning{} and
\AloneReasoning{}.

This delta appears in every domain. It is lowest for \AloneDropMinDomainName{} at \AloneDropMinDomain{} points and
highest for \AloneDropMaxDomainName{} at \AloneDropMaxDomain{} points. In the judgement cases, where we set the line
between the parties based upon our own judgement, the test is whether a model's single-file verdict is consistent with
its own view of the pair side by side.

Among the pairs a model without reasoning separates when it sees both cases, it gives the two parties the same answer
alone in \PartyNoDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoningJudgement{} of cases. The mean ``party difference''
(\S\ref{sec:measure}) falls from \PartyDiffSideBySideNoReasoningJudgement{} points to
\PartyDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoningJudgement{}. The models can state the requirement. They can also apply it when
another case is given as a reference. But models do not apply it to a single file. This matters because the single case
is the unit of decision in a real-world deployment.

\textbf{Milder conduct.} Where a model fails both parties alone, there are two explanations. Either the vignette we
created falls short of the lighter requirement, as the model reads it; or the model is ignoring who the party is. We
tried to distinguish them by changing one detail to make the conduct milder, e.g.\ an adviser reading out a fund's risk
description twice instead of once, with everything else left unchanged. Making the conduct milder freed the party held
to less in only \MilderPartySensitiveNoReasoning{} of these cases without reasoning, and \MilderPartySensitiveReasoning{}
with (Table~\ref{tab:milder}). In the rest of the cases, both parties still failed.

\textbf{School work.} The seventh domain evaluated the same one-page essay written by a ten-year-old or a
seventeen-year-old. The models split \RunTwoSixOneSideBySideNoReasoningEducation{} of pairs side by side without
reasoning and \RunTwoSixOneSideBySideReasoningEducation{} with it. Presented as single cases, they split
\RunTwoSixOneAloneNoReasoningEducation{} without reasoning and none of \RunTwoSixOneAloneReasoningEducationDen{} cells
with it (Table~\ref{tab:education}).

Models with reasoning split school-work pairs side by side less often than pairs in any other domain. When they did not
split a pair, they almost always failed both students (Appendix~\ref{app:later}). Without reasoning, the gap between
side by side and alone in school work is \EduGapNoReasoning{} points.

Among the school-work pairs a model splits side by side, it gives the two parties the same answer alone in
\EduNoDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoning{} of cases without reasoning and \EduNoDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoning{} with it, and
the mean party difference alone is \EduDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoning{} points and \EduDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoning{}.

In the judgement cases of the other domains, on the same configurations, the figures are
\SixJudgNoDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoning{} and \SixJudgNoDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoning{}, and
\SixJudgDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoning{} and \SixJudgDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoning{}. Reasoning recovers nearly all of
the difference for fixed rules, about half in the other judgement domains, and almost none in school work. With
\NumPairsEducation{} pairs, the school-work intervals are wide. The fixes by domain, with school work in its own column,
are in Tables~\ref{tab:by-domain-toldprinciple} to~\ref{tab:by-domain-example}.

The effect is present in the leading commercial models. Figure~\ref{fig:knows} shows the gap for each model in turn.
The largest is for \model{Claude Sonnet 5}, which separates \SideBySideClaudeSonnetFive{} of pairs side by side and
\AloneClaudeSonnetFive{} alone.

\textbf{Even fixed rules are problematic.} In fixed-rule cases a rule of law settles the answer, and the case states
the facts that decide it. Prima facie, these should be easy to resolve. Even so, models without reasoning decide only
\AloneNoReasoningFixedRule{} of pairs correctly alone.

These errors are not evenly spread. The party the rule protects is wrongly failed in
\HeldToLessWronglyFailedNoReasoning{} of cases, and the other party wrongly passed in
\OtherWronglyPassedNoReasoning{}. Most wrongly decided pairs fail both parties
(\WrongBothNotMetAmongWrongNoReasoningFixedRule{}), e.g.\ the householder and the small employer are judged by the
requirement the law places on the business and the large employer.

Models differ widely (Table~\ref{tab:wrong}): \model{Llama 3.3 70B} wrongly fails the party the rule protects in
\HeldToLessWronglyFailedLlamaSeventyB{} of cases, \model{GPT-6 Sol} in \HeldToLessWronglyFailedGptSixSol{}. On a single
file, the people a rule exists to protect are those the models most often get wrong.

\textbf{Order.} Even judging side by side, models are not reliable without reasoning. They split
\SideBySideHeldToLessFirst{} of pairs when the party held to less is listed first and \SideBySideHeldToLessSecond{}
when it is listed second. Position effects of this kind are well known in model judgements
\citep{zheng2023judging,wang2023fair}. Here this means that even the comparison that restores the requirement does so
only part of the time.

\subsection{Reasoning recovers verdicts against fixed rules, but not judgement}

\textbf{Reasoning, alone.} With reasoning, models perform well on fixed-rule cases, deciding
\AloneReasoningFixedRule{} of pairs correctly alone. This is close to the \SideBySideReasoningFixedRule{} they decide
side by side. In judgement cases they do not perform well: they separate the parties in
\SideBySideReasoningJudgement{} of pairs side by side, but in only \AloneReasoningJudgement{} when presented with

\PartyDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoningJudgement{} points, against \PartyDiffSideBySideReasoningJudgement{} side by side.

Reasoning narrows the gap, but most remains. One reading is that a rule stated in law is something a model can
retrieve and apply, while a requirement that needs judgement has nothing to retrieve, and the single file itself
supplies nothing to compare against.

\textbf{Asked directly.} The models know the requirements. Asked directly, they say the parties should be held to
different standards in \DirectYesRateNoReasoning{} of answers without reasoning and \DirectYesRateReasoning{} with it.
They also rate that difference as large. When asked to place it on a scale where zero means the same is expected of
both and nine means far more is expected of one party, the average answer is about \DirectDigitRounded{}.
With the parties in reverse order, \MirrorYesRateNoReasoning{} and \MirrorYesRateReasoning{} answered yes. The mean
rating fell to \MirrorDigitNoReasoning{} and \MirrorDigitReasoning{}.

Side by side they also apply the difference. Their failure to apply it to cases read alone is thus not a matter of
disagreeing with the requirement. Recalculating only the domains where a model says the parties should differ leaves
the judgement case figures when cases are presented alone unchanged, at
\FilterAloneSaysDifferNoReasoningJudgementSeven{} and \FilterAloneSaysDifferReasoningJudgementSeven{}
(Appendix~\ref{app:later}).

\textbf{Told in principle.} The first response of a prompt developer to failed tests of this kind is to state the
requirement in context. To replicate this, we added one sentence to each case saying, in general terms, how the
requirement differs between the two types of party. For example, a dismissal case began: ``A large employer is held to a
higher standard of procedure before dismissing someone than a small employer.'' Told in principle, models without reasoning split
\ToldPrincipleNoReasoningJudgement{} of judgement-case pairs, against \AloneNoReasoningJudgement{} untold. Models with
reasoning split \ToldPrincipleReasoningJudgement{}, against \AloneReasoningJudgement{}. Being told what it already knows
helps the model, but leaves it well short of its performance when it has a case to compare. Neither of these figures is
close to what a system deciding single files in a regulated domain would require without human review.

\textbf{The same model, with and without reasoning.} Reasoning raised the judgement-case figure alone for four of the
five models read both ways; \model{Claude Haiku 4.5} stayed at \AloneClaudeHaikuFourFiveReasoningJudgement{}
(Table~\ref{tab:both-ways}).

None of the models attained the same performance as its own side-by-side split. Reasoning thus narrows the gap but
does not close it.

The problem has not been solved in the frontier models, as might be expected. They show the same pattern, e.g.\
\model{Claude Opus 5.5} splits \SideBySideOpusFiveFiveDefaultEffortJudgement{} of judgement-case pairs when shown them

\subsection{A precise rule fixes the question it answers}
\textbf{The precise rule can restore the verdict.} Told a precise rule that settles the case by a stated fact, models
without reasoning split \ToldRuleNoReasoning{} of pairs alone. Models with reasoning reach
\ToldRuleReasoningJudgement{} in judgement cases. This approach does not work for every model: told the rule,
\model{Grok 4.3} without reasoning splits just \ToldRuleGrokFourThree{} of pairs, \model{Gemma 3 27B}
\ToldRuleGemmaTwentySevenB{} and \model{Mistral Small 3.2 24B} \ToldRuleMistralTwentyFourB{}.

\textbf{But it lets other failings through.} We added to the case of the ``party held to less'' an additional failing
the rule does not mention. Without the rule, models correctly judge such cases to fall short. With it, they accept the
failing in \LetThroughNoReasoning{} of cases without reasoning and \LetThroughReasoning{} with it
(Figure~\ref{fig:let-through}). Reasoning models perform worse than non-reasoning models. They follow the rule more
closely, and with the usual question that means letting more through.

\textbf{The question decides what the rule means.} On a random subset of \SecondWordingPairs{} pairs we tested a
second question, asking whether the party ``met all of its obligations''. That cut let-through from
\SecondWordingRuleOriginalNoReasoning{} to \SecondWordingRuleNewNoReasoning{} without reasoning
(\SecondWordingRuleChangeNoReasoning{} points [\SecondWordingRuleChangeNoReasoningLo{},
\SecondWordingRuleChangeNoReasoningHi{}]) and from \SecondWordingRuleOriginalReasoning{} to
\SecondWordingRuleNewReasoning{} with it (\SecondWordingRuleChangeReasoning{} [\SecondWordingRuleChangeReasoningLo{},
\SecondWordingRuleChangeReasoningHi{}]).

To check the impact of changing the question, as a control, we read the plain case of the ``party held to less'' with the rule
but without any added failing. With reasoning, it was still judged met in \SecondWordingControlPlainMetReasoning{} of
cases. This tells us that the broader question did not make reasoning models stricter, per se. This suggests that the
reasoning models did not read the rule as an exemption, they interpreted the original question as asking only whether
the rule was met.

Without reasoning, the plain case was judged met in only \SecondWordingControlPlainMetNoReasoning{}, so the broader
question trades let-through for failing the party held to less (Table~\ref{tab:wording}).

\textbf{Rule and example under the second wording.} Compared on the same subset, the precise rule let through
\SecondWordingRuleOriginalNoReasoning{} of added failings with the original question and
\SecondWordingRuleNewNoReasoning{} with the second wording without reasoning (\SecondWordingRuleOriginalReasoning{} and
\SecondWordingRuleNewReasoning{} with it). The worked example with a counter-example let through
\SecondWordingExampleOriginalNoReasoning{} and \SecondWordingExampleNewNoReasoning{}
(\SecondWordingExampleOriginalReasoning{} and \SecondWordingExampleNewReasoning{}). On the subset's
\RestoredSubsetPairs{} judgement pairs (school work included), the second wording also reduced how often the rule restored the verdict for the
party held to less: from \RestoredRuleOriginalNoReasoning{} to \RestoredRuleNewNoReasoning{} without reasoning, and
from \RestoredRuleOriginalReasoning{} to \RestoredRuleNewReasoning{} with it. The worked example, read only with the
original question, restored \RestoredExampleOriginalNoReasoning{} and \RestoredExampleOriginalReasoning{}. The example
was not read for the plain case under the second wording.

\textbf{Unrelated failings pass more often.} Failings on another subject from the rule's are let through more often
than failings on its own subject (\LetThroughOtherSubjectNoReasoning{} against \LetThroughSameSubjectNoReasoning{}
without reasoning; Table~\ref{tab:cutoff}).

\textbf{A worked example with a counterexample.} On every pair, school work included, we showed before each case a
decided case from the same domain but from another pair, along with a counterexample that marks its limit. The example
and the counterexample were always shown together in the full run, never separately.

With the example, models without reasoning split \ExampleSplitNoReasoning{} of pairs alone, against
\ExampleBareSplitNoReasoning{} with no example in the same session; models with reasoning \ExampleSplitReasoning{},
against \ExampleBareSplitReasoning{}.

The added failing was let through in \ExampleLetThroughNoReasoning{} of cases without reasoning and
\ExampleLetThroughReasoning{} with it (Figure~\ref{fig:let-through}), against \RunTwoSixOneLetThroughNoReasoningSevenDomains{} and
\RunTwoSixOneLetThroughReasoningSevenDomains{} for the precise rule on the same pairs and models.
Of the cases the example led a model to split, the failing was still rejected in \ExampleStillRejectedNoReasoning{} and
\ExampleStillRejectedReasoning{}, so the low let-through is not a model answering no to everything
(Table~\ref{tab:example}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\textbf{What should a deployer learn from this paper?} The configuration that is most likely to be used at production
volume is the configuration with the worst performance. Reasoning is often impractical in production deployments for
reasons of cost or speed. When case files are processed one at a time, models split just one pair in ten
(\AloneNoReasoning{}) successfully.

The errors flow one way only, towards over-blaming the ``party held to less''. In most domains, this means that the
weaker party is held to a stronger standard. This is the opposite of what the rule was created to achieve. Where a
rule of law settles the answer, \HeldToLessWronglyFailedNoReasoning{} of the parties it protects are wrongly failed.
Judgement cases perform even worse. Models fail the ``party held to less'' in \HeldNotMetAloneNoReasoningJudgement{}
of single files. They fail \HeldNotMetAloneSidePassedNoReasoningJudgement{} of those that they themselves passed when
they saw them side by side.

We created the shortest, cleanest cases possible, written to isolate the party. Our figures, though themselves stark,
are also a best case. Real files are longer and messier, and verdicts on them should be expected to be less accurate
and more impacted by noise.

A similar phenomenon appears in \citet{young2026blame}. The harshest reading prevailed unless something in view
relieved it, described as the standard of ``strict causal blame'', where mitigating factors are ignored.

\textbf{Why it happens.} One potential reading of the results is this: a fixed rule is something the model can
retrieve and apply to the facts. This is why reasoning recovers fixed rules (\AloneReasoningFixedRule{}, close to the
\SideBySideReasoningFixedRule{} side by side). A requirement that needs judgement is judged by reference to
something, but the single file supplies no reference point. This closely parallels Hsee's evaluability hypothesis on
human judgement \citep{hsee1996evaluability}. Without a point of comparison, models mostly fall back to the stricter
reading.

\textbf{Why the usual tests mislead.} Testing the model by asking it to state the requirement will pass. Comparing
two cases in context will also pass. Any test pack built upon these kinds of cases will certify a system that will
fail on single files.

\textbf{The usual fixes look like progress, but bring problems.} A precise rule makes the tested case pass, but it
often lets through other failings the test does not contain. The fix is brittle to the question: ``the standard that
applies'' against ``all of its obligations''.

Outcome monitoring by party type, which regulators expect, is designed to uncover discrimination: unjustly unequal
treatment. This failure is its reverse: unjustly equal treatment. It can pass those checks unseen, making it a silent
failure.

\textbf{What works as a fix, and what doesn't.}

\emph{Fixed rules.} Reasoning largely solves fixed rules (\AloneReasoningFixedRule{} alone), and stating the rule
brings reasoning models to \ToldRuleReasoningFixedRule{}.

\emph{Judgement:}
\begin{itemize}
\item A precise rule, with reasoning and the broader question, comes closest (\RestoredRuleNewReasoning{} of verdicts
restored and \SecondWordingRuleNewReasoning{} of failings let through, on a subset). But it needs a rule written in
advance for each case, and edge cases nobody anticipated fall back to unaided judgement
(\AloneReasoningJudgement{}). Moreover, principles-based regulation exists partly to avoid maintaining large sets of rules
with unpredictable gaps.
\item Comparison works best without a rule (\SideBySideReasoningJudgement{} side by side, with reasoning). But it
would mean constructing an arbitrary and fictitious counterpart for each real file, such as the same HR case with a
different employer. This approach is hard to defend as the basis of a decision in a regulated setting.
\item A worked example with a matching counterexample lets almost no failings through (\ExampleLetThroughNoReasoning{}
without reasoning and \ExampleLetThroughReasoning{} with). But this approach only closes about half of the gap between
single files and side by side. In judgement cases, pairs split alone rise from \AloneNoReasoningJudgement{} to
\ExampleSplitNoReasoningJudgement{} without reasoning, against \SideBySideNoReasoningJudgement{} side by side, and
from \AloneReasoningJudgement{} to \ExampleSplitReasoningJudgement{} with it, against
\SideBySideReasoningJudgement{}.
\item Stating the principle adds little.
\end{itemize}
We conclude that no single fix provides a complete solution for single-file requirements that need judgement. A
combination may do better: reasoning, precise rules where situations can be anticipated, worked examples for the rest,
and a question that asks about all of the party's obligations. Without reasoning, the broader question fails the party
held to less instead. We did not test this combination.

\textbf{Rules and standards.} Regulators often prefer principles to detailed rules, partly because rules invite
creative compliance \citep{black2007making}. The UK Financial Conduct Authority's Consumer Duty is a recent example: it
states general principles of fairness, not detailed rules.

In our experience as deployers of AI systems, the incentives placed on developers are counterproductive. Developers
are given a marked test set by the business, and their goal is to pass it and get the system into production. This
strongly incentivises fitting the prompt to the test set, even where that is counterproductive outside it. In our
tests, writing the judgement into the prompt as a rule raised the tested cases from \AloneReasoningJudgement{} to
\ToldRuleReasoningJudgement{} for reasoning models in judgement cases, while letting through \LetThroughReasoningJudgement{} of failings the rule did not
mention. A test pack would not show those failings.

This is Goodhart's law at work \citep{goodhart1975problems,strathern1997improving}. Once a measure becomes a target, it
ceases to be a good measure of performance.

\textbf{What deployers can do now.} Whether to use reasoning is usually driven by cost and speed, but, where a
decision turns on a fixed rule, reasoning recovers most single-file verdicts (\AloneReasoningFixedRule{}). Without
reasoning, stating the rule in the prompt is the cheaper alternative, but it restores fewer
(\ToldRuleNoReasoningFixedRule{}). Take care with the question. Where reasoning is used, ask whether the party met all
of its obligations, not whether it met ``the standard''. Single-file verdicts on a requirement that needs judgement
should not stand without human review, with or without reasoning. The errors fall on ``not met'' for the party held
to less, so those are the verdicts to route to a reviewer, though we did not test such a workflow.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Our cases are constructed as short vignettes intentionally written to isolate the party. Real files -- email chains,
case files, essays, customer interactions -- are likely to be far longer and padded with large amounts of irrelevant
material. This implies that these results should be read as a best case.

In the judgement cases, we set the line between meeting and failing the requirement when designing the vignettes. The
party difference measure, the models' own answers when asked directly and the models' own high scores for the
effect all show that the models agree the parties should differ in treatment. But where the line falls has not been
tested against practitioners. We have intentionally not measured any human baseline, so we do not claim models do worse
than human reviewers. We claim that the models' behaviour is not consistent with their own strongly held position on
what that behaviour should be. Note that the judgement cases closely parallel the fixed-rule cases, where there is a fact of
the matter.

All cases are set in UK law, which raises the possibility that the models are applying the wrong jurisdiction. We tested
this by adding ``UK law applies'' but this changed no group figure.

The precise rules in the judgement domains are stipulated, not statements of law. The second wording was read on
\SecondWordingPairs{} of the \NumPairsAllSeven{} pairs. The provider that served \model{Kimi K3} in the first session
stopped serving it without reasoning by the second session. That is why its later reads came from another provider and
are reported apart.

We tested yes-or-no verdicts -- not graded scores -- because yes/no is the final verdict in such cases. It is thus the
ultimate question at stake. Whether graded scores show the same gap was tested only in
exploratory runs (Supplementary~\ref{app:exploratory}).

A model may lean towards the ``party held to less'' without changing its verdict. The party difference does show a lean
of this kind (\PartyDiffAloneGivenSplitNoReasoningJudgement{} points without reasoning,
\PartyDiffAloneGivenSplitReasoningJudgement{} with).

But making the conduct one degree milder rarely changed the verdict, suggesting that few verdicts lay close to the
threshold. Side by side, the case labels named the attribute that differs between the parties. We did not test neutral
labels, which could make that difference less salient to the models.

Reasoning models were read with fewer answers per prompt, and some models cannot have reasoning switched off or its
amount fixed. Results describe the models as served on the dates given.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}
The cases are fictional and contain no personal data. The work identifies a way in which automated decisions can treat
parties unfairly; we release the cases and responses so that deployers can test their own configurations. The cases
could in principle be used to tune a system to pass these tests without improving its judgement; we think the benefit
of open testing outweighs that risk.

AI assistance was used in designing the study, building the code and analysing the results. The first drafts of the

\paragraph{Intervals.} Every interval in the paper comes from one bootstrap design. The unit is a cell: one model's
reading of one pair under one condition. Its repeated draws (ten without reasoning, five with) are reduced to a yes-rate,
and the cell enters the bootstrap whole; draws within a cell are not resampled. In each of \BootstrapReplicates{}
replicates, pairs are resampled with replacement within their domain, keeping each domain's number of pairs, and, where
a figure pools more than one model configuration, the configurations are resampled with replacement as well,
independently of the pairs. A replicate's share is the sum of its resampled cells' numerators over the sum of their
denominators. The interval runs from the \BootstrapLowPctile{} to the \BootstrapHighPctile{} percentile of the
replicates. Pairs are drawn with seed \BootstrapSeedPairs{} and configurations with seed \BootstrapSeedModels{} (one set
of draws for each set of configurations, so two figures over the same models share their draws and their difference
stays paired). Where an interval has zero width, the Wilson score interval for the count replaces it. A figure for one
model resamples pairs only. Every caption's ``bootstrap intervals'' means this design.

\section{Exploratory findings}
\label{app:exploratory}
Before the confirmatory run, a series of discovery runs shaped its design. None is a registered result, every figure
below is exploratory, and the bases are small. Most of these runs scored each case on a graded scale (a digit from
zero to nine unless stated) rather than asking yes or no. Their measure is the \emph{share}: the gap between the two
parties alone divided by the gap side by side, the median over models. A gap \emph{holds} alone on a model when it is
above that model's noise floor, measured with neutral sentences and day-of-the-week controls. Each run's
pre-registration, frames, responses, results and outcomes are in \RepoWhere{} under the path given.

\textbf{People, animals, eras and organisations (exploratory; run~\ExplRunGradingVariations{},
\url{discovery/run252/}).} Pieces of work were scored for parties of different kinds, \ExplSubjectTestPairs{} test
pairs and one control per kind, on \ExplGradingVarModels{} models without reasoning, \ExplDiscoveryDraws{} draws per
prompt. For a child against an older student, the models kept \ExplFormatDigitShare{} of the side-by-side gap alone.
For animals (a puppy against an experienced dog) they kept \ExplSubjectAnimalsShare{} (holding on
\ExplSubjectAnimalsHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{} models), and for things of different eras
\ExplSubjectErasShare{} (\ExplSubjectErasHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{}); one of the two era pairs was less
plausible and kept less. Organisations largely kept the difference alone: \ExplSubjectOrganisationsShare{}, holding on
\ExplSubjectOrganisationsHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{} models, with the small organisation marked up and the
large one marked down. So the loss alone was not specific to people, but organisations rated on their work kept most
of the difference. In the main study, organisations held to a requirement (employers, firms and companies) lost the
difference alone under yes-or-no verdicts; the exploratory run rated organisations on the quality of their work, on a
graded scale.

\textbf{Grading and other domains (exploratory; runs~\ExplRunAbsentComparison{} and~\ExplRunStandardsSurvey{},
\url{discovery/run248/}, \url{discovery/run251/}).} Run~\ExplRunAbsentComparison{} read \ExplAbsentDomains{} domains,
\ExplAbsentPairsPerDomain{} pairs each, on \ExplAbsentModelsOff{} models without reasoning (\ExplDiscoveryDraws{}
draws) and \ExplAbsentModelsReasoning{} with it (\ExplAbsentDrawsReasoning{} draws). Without reasoning, the models kept
alone \ExplAbsentGradingShareOff{} of the gap in grading, \ExplAbsentEffortShareOff{} in effort,
\ExplAbsentProportionalityShareOff{} in proportionality, \ExplAbsentPricesShareOff{} in prices and
\ExplAbsentPerformanceShareOff{} in performance; with reasoning, \ExplAbsentGradingShareReasoning{},
\ExplAbsentEffortShareReasoning{}, \ExplAbsentProportionalityShareReasoning{}, \ExplAbsentPricesShareReasoning{} and
\ExplAbsentPerformanceShareReasoning{}. Run~\ExplRunStandardsSurvey{} surveyed \ExplSurveyDomains{} domains with
\ExplSurveyPairsPerDomain{} pairs each on \ExplGradingVarModels{} models without reasoning (\ExplDiscoveryDraws{}
draws). Seniority was the clearest loss: every model held the senior manager to the stricter standard side by side,
but alone kept \ExplSurveySeniorityShare{} of the gap, holding on \ExplSurveySeniorityHolds{} of
\ExplGradingVarModels{} models. Vulnerable customers (\ExplSurveyVulnerableShare{}, \ExplSurveyVulnerableHolds{} of
\ExplGradingVarModels{}) and grading (\ExplSurveyGradingShare{}, \ExplSurveyGradingHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{})
kept more on a graded scale. These runs informed the choice of the confirmatory domains.

\textbf{Response formats (exploratory; runs~\ExplRunGradingVariations{} and~\ExplRunVerdicts{},
\url{discovery/run252/}, \url{discovery/run253/}).} Run~\ExplRunGradingVariations{} read \ExplFormatTestPairs{} essay
pairs (a child against an older student) on the same \ExplGradingVarModels{} models in six formats. Graded scales kept
part of the gap alone: a digit \ExplFormatDigitShare{}, a mark out of a hundred \ExplFormatMarksShare{} and a letter
grade \ExplFormatLetterShare{}, each holding on \ExplFormatDigitHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{} models; a
one-word rating kept \ExplFormatWordsShare{} (\ExplFormatWordsHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{}). A pass-or-fail
decision kept none: alone, the older student's borderline essay passed, and the gap held on
\ExplFormatPassFailHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{} models. A yes-or-no decision to publish kept
\ExplFormatDecisionShare{} (\ExplFormatDecisionHolds{} of \ExplGradingVarModels{}). Run~\ExplRunVerdicts{} then asked
for a yes-or-no verdict on whether the standard was met, on \ExplVerdictTestPairs{} test pairs in
\ExplVerdictDomains{} domains, \ExplVerdictModels{} models, \ExplDiscoveryDraws{} draws. Alone, the verdict lost the
split in \ExplVerdictHoldsNoneDomains{} of the \ExplVerdictDomains{} domains on every model and held on at most
\ExplVerdictHoldsMax{} models elsewhere (grading); both parties usually got the same answer, most often ``no''. The
confirmatory run asks for the yes-or-no verdict, the format closest to a decision.

\textbf{Session and route drift (exploratory; runs~\ExplRunOrgVariations{} and~\ExplRunScaleCeiling{},
\url{discovery/run254/}, \url{discovery/run255/}).} Before run~\ExplRunOrgVariations{}, \model{Claude Sonnet 5} was
read directly through its provider's interface on \ExplRouteCheckCells{} cells from the previous day's run, which had
gone through a routing service, \ExplDiscoveryDraws{} draws each. Only \ExplRouteCheckMatch{} of
\ExplRouteCheckCells{} cells matched. Before run~\ExplRunScaleCeiling{}, the same cells were read again by both routes:
the routing service matched its earlier draws on \ExplRouteTriangleMatchBanked{} of \ExplRouteCheckCells{} cells and
agreed with the direct route on \ExplRouteTriangleMatchDirect{}; \ExplRouteTriangleOvernight{} cell had changed overnight on both
routes. Answers to identical prompts can therefore shift between routes to the same model and between days. This is
why every model in the confirmatory run read every condition in a single session through a single route
(\S\ref{sec:method}).

draws) and two with it (\ExplExampleOnlyDrawsReasoning{} draws), with the worked example shown without its
counter-example. It split \ExplExampleOnlySplitNoReasoningAllNum{} of \ExplExampleOnlySplitNoReasoningAllDen{} cells
alone without reasoning, against \ExplExampleOnlyBareSplitNoReasoningAllNum{} of
\ExplExampleOnlyBareSplitNoReasoningAllDen{} with no example, and let \ExplExampleOnlyLetThroughNoReasoningAllNum{} of
\ExplExampleOnlyLetThroughNoReasoningAllDen{} added failings through. With reasoning, it split
\ExplExampleOnlySplitReasoningAllNum{} of \ExplExampleOnlySplitReasoningAllDen{} against
\ExplExampleOnlyBareSplitReasoningAllNum{} of \ExplExampleOnlyBareSplitReasoningAllDen{}, with
\ExplExampleOnlyLetThroughReasoningAllNum{} of \ExplExampleOnlyLetThroughReasoningAllDen{} let through.

\textbf{The example read as a precedent (exploratory; run~\ExplRunExampleOnly{}, \url{discovery/run262/}).} In the
same run, whose reasoning we kept (the later session did not collect it), one reasoning model, \model{Claude Haiku
4.5}, read the example as a precedent: it stated the principle the example decided, asked whether the new case fell
within it, and distinguished it when it judged that it did not. This sometimes left the pair not split, when it judged
the requirement the party held to less was excused from to be one it still bore.
